Client Role

My site was built around my personal coding projects. I knew what I wanted: a clean, technical portfolio that actually showed my work. For the visuals, I used AI to generate custom images with specific instructions so everything was copyright-free and matched the aesthetic I had in mind.

Communication with my lead developer Nickolas Hawley was straightforward. No issues. He did what he was supposed to do, the site came together, and it fit my purposes. I checked in periodically and the result was acceptable.

If I hired a dev team again, I'd front-load the communication more. Specific deadlines per section, not just a general "let me know when you're done." Clearer checkpoints would prevent the passive waiting that slows things down.

My site: hawlyway.github.io/wdd130-isaacm

Lead Developer Role

I was the lead developer for Sadie Armstrong's site, The Perfect Rom Com Guide. I built the shared style.css file from scratch. Open Sans for body text, Permanent Marker for headings, sage green (#B6CEBC) header and footer, light card sections (#E0E0E0) with rounded corners, and a centered 1000px max-width layout. The goal was a clean, readable site that matched her site plan.

I assigned Nathan Cross and Seth Jones their pages through GitHub with clear instructions on which page to work on, what content to use, and how to link the shared stylesheet. I kept it simple and direct. There were no GitHub conflicts and the push/pull workflow stayed clean throughout.

I sent Sadie the live URL after pushing changes and asked for feedback. Communication was functional. The main issue was that my junior developers did not fully complete their pages, so the site never reached the state it could have.

Sadie's site: zephyrusis.github.io/wdd130-armstrong

Junior Developer Role

I worked as a junior developer on two teams. Ben Clark gave me clear instructions on which page to work on, how to access the repo, and what was expected. I pulled the latest changes before making edits, completed my page, and pushed without any conflicts. No issues there.

Nathan Cross's team was a different story. He never added me to the repository, never reached out, and never provided any direction. I checked in when I could but nothing came back. I had a page assigned to me with no way to actually access or contribute to the project. That is a gap I could not fill on my own.

Pearl.html (Ben Clark's team): benclark123.github.io — pearl.html

General Experience and Reflections

The hardest part was dealing with teammates who did not communicate or follow through. You can only do so much when someone does not respond and does not add you to their repo. That kind of gap is not something you can close through effort alone. It requires the other person to show up.

What I enjoyed most was the lead developer role. Building the CSS from scratch, making real decisions about the design, and seeing the site come together was satisfying. That part felt like actual development work.

If I did this again, I would set hard check-in deadlines from day one. Not optional check-ins, required ones. I would also document everything so there is a clear record of who did what. Accountability gaps get covered up too easily without that.

After this project, yes, I am more confident working in a real-world team structure. I know how to manage a shared repository, coordinate styles across pages, and communicate task expectations clearly. I also know that team output is only as strong as the weakest link, and planning for that is part of the job.
